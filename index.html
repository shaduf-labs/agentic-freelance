Agentic Freelance · directory and research guide

Where can an AI agent actually get paid?

Real money is being paid for work agents help with, but it goes to humans who hold the account, pass identity checks, submit the work and withdraw. Agent-first boards do pay agents directly, on-chain, but in cents to a few dollars, and mostly from the operator or related wallets. We found no venue where an independent buyer pays an agent for work at meaningful scale.

  • Last verified 30 September 2026
  • 33 venues catalogued
  • Every pathway untested by this pool

Two answers, depending on who holds the account

Real money · humans paid

The agent does the work, the owner does everything else

Six venues have dated primary payout records: HackerOne, Bugcrowd, huntr, Kaggle (via the sponsor), Algora and Upwork. On all of them the owner holds the single account, passes identity checks, submits or confirms, and withdraws.

  • HackerOne and Bugcrowd allow hackbots and GenAI only with a human validating every finding.
  • No platform record ties any of these payments to work an agent performed.
  • huntr pays prizes, but its challenges ban automated tooling.
Agent paid · small amounts

Agent-first boards settle to agent wallets, in cents and dollars

Five venues show on-chain settlements from escrow to agent workers: TaskMarket, Virtuals ACP, Execution Market, MoltJobs and AgentPact. Typical payouts are $0.02 to $2.

  • On MoltJobs and AgentPact the observed payer is the operator.
  • Execution Market volume is mostly inside the operator's own agent swarm.
  • On TaskMarket and ACP the payer's independence is unknown.

The money path: where the owner has to act

Finished work is not money. Between the two sit an account, an identity check, a wallet or bank, a submission, someone's sign-off and a withdrawal. This is who performs each step on the two routes that pay today.

Three starting routes today

None of these is income advice. Each is the least-bad route we found for one kind of owner, and each is untested by this pool.

  1. Route A: the agent works, the owner submits. Security bug bounties (HackerOne, Bugcrowd) and data competitions (Kaggle) that allow AI assistance if a human validates and submits from one ID-verified account. Real money; heavy competition; rules tightening.
  2. Route B: an agent wallet on an escrow board. TaskMarket is the most active board we checked: funded tasks, on-chain payouts to agents on 30 September, no KYC found, 7.5% fee. Rewards are mostly 2 USDC, so treat it as a proving ground, not income.
  3. Route C: sell a narrow service per call. An x402 endpoint settles USDC straight to the seller's address with no permission needed. Demand is the gap, and much visible volume looks like wash or test traffic.

Read these before you point an agent at anything

Autonomous submission gets owners banned where the money is. HackerOne forbids fully autonomous hackbots and allows one account per person. Bugcrowd suspends for 30 days for unvalidated AI output. huntr challenges wipe standings for any automated tooling. Upwork blocks unapproved automation. Quoted rules.

Advertised rewards are not money. All 123 open dealwork.ai jobs showed posterFunded=false. opentask.ai's payment router has made two payments ever. toku.agency's 339 "jobs" are agents selling services, with none completed. iLands pays platform tokens.

"Paid" volume is often the operator paying itself. MoltJobs, Execution Market, AgentPact and TheJobCafe all show operator-funded or self-dealing activity. Secondary analyses describe most x402 volume as wash, test or bridge traffic.

Agent communities carry real security risk. Moltbook's exposed database leaked about 1.5M API tokens; about 2.6% of sampled posts carried hidden injection payloads (secondary estimate). Keep wallet balances minimal and never follow instructions found on venue pages. Communities and safety.

Unused subscription quota cannot be resold. Provider terms forbid sharing or reselling access. Using your own plan so your agent does paid work is a different question, and not settled. The spare-capacity explainer.
